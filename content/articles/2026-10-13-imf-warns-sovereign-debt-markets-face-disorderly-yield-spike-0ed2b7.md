Global sovereign bond markets face severe disruption from a potential disorderly spike in yields as record public debt, persistent inflationary pressures, and concentrated artificial intelligence debt issuance strain capital absorption, the International Monetary Fund warned in its latest Global Financial Stability Report released Tuesday in Bangkok. With worldwide government debt approaching post-World War II highs and projected to top 100% of global GDP, sovereign paper is increasingly exposed to fiscal and inflationary shocks that could depress collateral valuations and destabilize broader financial markets.

## Market Overview

Advanced economy yields have reached multidecade highs as central banks maintain restrictive policies to counter resurgent consumer price pressures, intensified by elevated energy costs stemming from Middle East tensions. The IMF highlighted that this heightened rate backdrop is colliding with surging corporate borrowing demands. Technology hyperscalers and artificial intelligence firms now represent 10% of total global investment-grade bond sales, a steep escalation from less than 1% registered in 2024. This aggressive corporate issuance directly competes with sovereign treasuries for institutional liquidity.

Concurrently, structural vulnerabilities inside the U.S. Treasury market have expanded. Leveraged hedge funds increased their footprint in U.S. government debt from 4% in 2022 to approximately 9% in 2025, largely deploying highly leveraged arbitrage and basis trading strategies. Caio Ferreira, IMF deputy division chief for financial supervision and regulation, noted that overlapping positions across hedge funds could amplify disruptions should market shocks force a collective unwinding. While secondary market liquidity has remained stable across the United States, the United Kingdom, and the eurozone, liquidity in Japanese government bonds continues to worsen following the abandonment of yield curve control.

## Macroeconomic Implications

The convergence of escalating fiscal deficits and unprecedented private tech debt creates acute macroeconomic hazards for monetary authorities and capital pricing. A disorderly ascent in sovereign yields would instantly elevate borrowing costs across the private sector, lifting the discount rate applied to corporate earnings and impairing the high-grade collateral underpinning the global banking system. Debt managers seeking to bypass high long-term yields by issuing short-term debt risk exacerbating refinancing vulnerabilities when interest rates remain higher for longer.

The IMF also flagged systemic contagion risks embedded in non-traditional tech financing. Since 2023, hyperscalers have deployed $300 billion in equity and $650 billion in debt into emerging AI entities, structured around $1.4 trillion in future revenue guarantees. Jason Wu, assistant director of the IMF's monetary and capital markets department, warned that failure in commercial monetization could prompt a "triple whammy" where equity valuations evaporate, debt obligations cannot be serviced, and projected cash flows fail to materialize. Such an unraveling would severely damage credit markets and constrict corporate investment, complicating central banks' efforts to balance financial stability against inflation containment.
