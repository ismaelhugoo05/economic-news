Federal import levies added 2.9 percentage points to prices across 67 product categories through February 2026, offsetting what would have otherwise been a deflationary trend in consumer goods, according to the Federal Reserve Bank of New York. The researchers concluded that absent these trade restrictions, retail prices across the surveyed goods would have dropped by nearly 1%, providing clear evidence of policy-driven price pressure on domestic households.

## Market Overview

The study, authored by New York Fed economists Mary Amiti, Sebastian Heise, and David Weinstein, reveals how border duties filter directly into domestic retail channels. Contrary to assertions that foreign exporters would absorb the financial burden, researchers found a pass-through rate of roughly 26%. Specifically, each single percentage point advance in the average tariff rate translated into a 0.25 percentage point rise in retail prices after a one-year lag.

The transmission mechanism extended beyond primary import costs. Direct duties accounted for approximately two-thirds of the total price impact, while the remaining third emerged through indirect channels, such as elevated operating costs for domestic firms that depend on imported parts and raw materials. Although the pace of annual price growth across these goods peaked in early 2026, researchers noted that consumer price levels are projected to remain structurally elevated through 2027.

## Macroeconomic Implications

These findings demonstrate that tariffs disrupted a broader baseline deflation in goods, presenting friction for monetary policymakers monitoring consumer price pressures. The evidence shows that domestic buyers, rather than foreign counterparties, shouldered a significant portion of the duty expenses.

Trade policy continues to face structural uncertainty. A February Supreme Court decision struck down significant portions of the original tariff regime, requiring billions of dollars in duty refunds to domestic retailers. Nevertheless, the White House is deploying alternative authorities to sustain commercial barriers, with many foreign products now facing import rates around 10%. Although this represents a downward adjustment from earlier duty levels, the persistent transmission mechanisms identified by the central bank indicate that revised tariffs will continue to exert upward pressure on retail price baselines.
